Who counts as a case

Class

Author

Joshua Loftus

Note

This is the student version of the class sheet. The exercises are for your own time; the class works through everything under “In class”. Answers are revealed in class.

How this sheet works

Exercises rederive or apply the lecture’s results. Do them on your own time; they are not worked in class. The class works the discussions, the problems and the coding under “In class,” in that order. Discussions are in groups of three or four, then with the room. Each problem, and the coding, is gone through as soon as the room has tried it.

Every item is about one decision: who is in the data, what is recorded about them, and what to do about the people and the columns that are not there. Someone made that decision, usually not the people left out, and every way of handling what is missing is an assumption about people nobody asked. So each item ends by asking who the mistake falls on, who gains from it going unnoticed, or who the choice could be justified to. The arithmetic is how you earn an answer to that question; it is not the answer.

Notation as in the lecture: \(C = 1\) if a person’s \(Y\) is recorded and \(C = 0\) if it is missing; \(e(X) = P(C = 1 \mid X)\); \(A\) is a group. A subscript on an expectation names the random variables it averages over: \(E_X\big[E_Y[Y \mid X]\big]\) averages \(Y\) among the people with each value of \(X\), then averages those means over \(X\). An expectation with no subscript averages over everything random inside it.

Attempt each part before you open a hint.

Exercise 1. Selection on a collider, by hand

A company’s records cover the people it hired. It hired every applicant who either did well at the interview or had a referral from a current employee. Among applicants, half did well at the interview and half had a referral, independently of each other.

  1. Among the people hired, what fraction of those with a referral did well at the interview? What fraction of those without a referral?

  2. Draw the causal graph with three nodes: interview, referral, hired. Which node do the company’s records condition on?

  3. The company notices that referred hires did worse at interview than other hires, concludes that referrals attract weaker candidates, and raises the interview bar for referred applicants. What is wrong with the conclusion, and who pays for it?

  4. Redo (a) when a fraction \(q\) of applicants do well at the interview, whatever \(q\) is. Does the gap between the two fractions depend on \(q\)?

TipHint 1

Start with the hires who have no referral. How did each of them get in?

TipHint 2

Among the applicants with a referral, the hiring rule kept everyone.

Exercise 2. The unaware model, with a smaller coaching effect

Leaving a protected characteristic out of a model is often done in the name of fairness. This exercise computes what it buys, in the lecture’s model with a smaller coaching effect.

Job performance \(Y \sim N(0, 1)\), larger is better. A group \(A \in \{0, 1\}\), half the population each, independent of \(Y\). A test score \(X = Y + A + \varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon \sim N(0, 1)\) independent of both: group 1 gets one point from coaching, and nothing else. (The lecture had two points, \(\beta_A = 2\); here \(\beta_A = 1\).)

  1. Compute \(E[X]\), \(\operatorname{Var}(X)\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)\). The unaware model is the regression of \(Y\) on \(X\): give its prediction \(\hat Y\) as a function of \(X\), and the average prediction in each group.

  2. The full model uses \(X\) and \(A\). Within group \(a\), \(X - a = Y + \varepsilon\). Give the full model’s prediction, the coefficient on \(A\), and the average prediction in each group.

  3. Compute the mean squared error of each model. Which is more accurate, and which predicts a bigger gap between the groups?

  4. Someone reads the coefficient on \(A\) in (b) as the effect of group membership on job performance. What is wrong with that reading? Which group does each of the two mistakes, the unaware model’s gap and this reading, fall on?

TipHint 1

The slope of a simple regression is covariance over variance, and the line passes through the point of means.

TipHint 2

For (c), the mean squared error of the regression of \(Y\) on \(X\) is \(\operatorname{Var}(Y) - \operatorname{Cov}(X, Y)^2/\operatorname{Var}(X)\).

Exercise 3. Inverse probability weighting

\(X\) is always recorded and \(Y\) is sometimes missing; \(C = 1\) when it is recorded, and \(e(X) = P(C = 1 \mid X) > 0\) for every value of \(X\).

  1. Show that under MAR, \(E\!\left[\dfrac{C\,Y}{e(X)}\right] = E[Y]\). Say where MAR is used, and where the condition \(e(X) > 0\) is used.

  2. In the lecture’s income example, \(X\) is age (young or old, half each), the young earn 40 on average and answer with probability 0.8, the old earn 60 on average and answer with probability 0.4. Compute the weighted mean and compare it with the mean of the recorded incomes, 46.7.

  3. In practice \(e(X)\) is not known. Say in one sentence how you would estimate it from the data, and what the estimate uses that the complete-case mean throws away.

  4. The weighted mean assumes that the old people who did not answer earn what the old people who did answer earn. The complete-case mean assumes something too: say what. Which assumption would you rather defend to the people who did not answer, and what would it take to check either?

TipHint 1

Condition on \(X\) first: \(E[CY/e(X)] = E_X\big[E_{C,Y}[CY \mid X]/e(X)\big]\).

TipHint 2

Under MAR, \(C\) and \(Y\) are independent given \(X\), so \(E_{C,Y}[CY \mid X] = E_C[C \mid X]\,E_Y[Y \mid X]\).

In class

Discussion 1: who is missing from the data you brought

Use the dataset or study you brought, where some people who should be in it are not. If you did not bring one, choose: a customer satisfaction survey; a hospital’s records of patient outcomes; a university’s record of its graduates’ salaries; the crimes recorded by the police; a dating app’s record of which matches led to a date.

  1. Who is missing, and which way does their absence push the result? Who is better off for that?
  2. Relative to what the dataset records about people, is the missingness closer to completely at random, at random, or not at random? What would the dataset have to record to make it “at random”?
  3. Name one dataset that should exist and does not. Who would have had to collect it, and why have they not?

Problem 1. Why is it missing, and which way?

  1. For each setting, say whether the missing values are MCAR, MAR or MNAR, and whether the mean of the recorded values is too high, too low, or neither, compared with the mean for everyone. Say which clause of the setting your answer depends on, and who is worse off if the recorded mean is taken at face value.
  • A fitness watch records resting heart rate only on nights it is worn. People take it off on nights they feel unwell, and nothing else about them is recorded.
  • Exam marks are missing for students whose exam scripts were lost in the post; the post lost one sack at random.
  • A survey asks about weekly hours worked, and the overall mean will set one staffing standard for both regions. Response is lower in one region than another, the region is recorded, and within each region whether someone responds is unrelated to their hours.

  1. A company surveys its staff about pay. Half are junior, with mean salary 30 (thousands), and half are senior, with mean salary 70. Juniors respond with probability 0.9 and seniors with probability 0.3. Within each grade, whether someone responds is unrelated to their salary. What is the true mean salary? What is the mean among respondents? Check your answer with \(E[Y \mid C = 1] - E[Y] = \operatorname{Cov}(Y, C)/P(C = 1)\).

  2. Weight each respondent by one over their grade’s response probability. Show that the weighted mean recovers the true mean. (For 100 staff: how many respondents are in each grade, and what weight does each get?)

  3. Now suppose that within each grade the better-paid staff respond less often. Is the weighted mean from (c) still right? Which way is it wrong, and what would fix it? Who would have to agree to the fix?

TipHint 1

For (b): \(E[YC]\) is the average over everyone of salary times the response indicator, so sum salary times response probability over the two grades, each with weight \(1/2\).

TipHint 2

For (c): of 100 staff, 50 are junior and 45 of them respond.

Discussion 2: why leave out \(A\)?

In the lecture’s test-score example, the model that leaves the group \(A\) out is less accurate in both groups and predicts a gap between groups whose \(Y\) has the same distribution.

  1. Write the strongest argument for leaving \(A\) out, and the strongest argument against. Then say which causal graph each argument assumes.
  2. Now \(A\) is not a protected characteristic but a column that was recorded for half the applicants. Would you drop the column, drop the rows that lack it, or fill it in? Who would you have to justify the choice to, and could you?

Coding 1: what a single fill-in does

You need ggplot2 and, for coding 2, mice (install.packages("mice") once). \(Y\) depends on \(X\). Half the values of \(Y\) are removed completely at random, so the complete cases are right about everything, and then each hole is filled once: with the recorded mean, and with the prediction from \(X\).

library(ggplot2)
n <- 500
X <- rnorm(n)
Y <- 1 + X + rnorm(n)                  # the truth: slope 1, sd(Y) = sqrt(2), cor(X, Y) = 1/sqrt(2)
C <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)                 # 1 if Y is recorded, 0 if it is missing: MCAR
d <- data.frame(X = X, Y = ifelse(C, Y, NA))
mean_imp <- ifelse(C, Y, mean(d$Y, na.rm = TRUE))     # every hole gets the recorded mean
reg_imp  <- # ...                                      # every hole gets the prediction from X
pic <- rbind(data.frame(X, Y = Y,        how = "all the data",          missing = C == 0),
             data.frame(X, Y = mean_imp, how = "mean imputation",       missing = C == 0),
             data.frame(X, Y = reg_imp,  how = "regression imputation", missing = C == 0))
ggplot(pic, aes(X, Y, color = missing)) + geom_point(alpha = 0.6) + facet_wrap(~ how) +
  scale_color_manual(values = c("gray50", "#BCAF6F"), labels = c("recorded", "missing"), name = NULL)
summaries <- function(y, x = X) c(sd = sd(y), cor = cor(x, y),
                                  slope = unname(coef(lm(y ~ x))[2]), q90 = unname(quantile(y, 0.9)))
round(rbind(`all the data` = summaries(Y), `complete cases` = summaries(Y[C == 1], X[C == 1]),
            `mean imputation` = summaries(mean_imp), `regression imputation` = summaries(reg_imp)), 2)
  1. Complete the regression-imputation line: fit lm(Y ~ X, data = d), which uses the rows that have \(Y\), and give each hole its fitted value. Run everything. In each panel the gold points are the rows whose \(Y\) went missing: on the left where they really are, in the other two where the fill-in put them. Say where they sit.
  2. Read the table against the truth: \(\operatorname{sd}(Y) = \sqrt2 \approx 1.41\), correlation \(1/\sqrt2 \approx 0.71\), slope \(1\), 90th percentile about \(2.81\). Which quantities does each fill-in get wrong, and which way? The missingness is completely at random, so what does filling in add here?
  3. Regression imputation gets the slope right and the correlation wrong. Say why, one sentence each.
  4. The filled-in rows are people who were not asked, drawn as if they had answered exactly as predicted. Who is better off when a dataset looks more certain than it is, and who finds out?

Coding 2: filling in, four ways

\(Y\) depends on \(X\) as in coding 1, and now values of \(Y\) are removed in three ways; the mean of \(Y\) is then estimated four ways. The regression-imputation line is the one you wrote in coding 1.

library(mice)
n <- 2000
X <- rnorm(n)
Y <- 1 + X + rnorm(n)                       # the truth: E[Y] = 1, slope 1
C_mcar <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)                 # 1 if Y is recorded: with probability 1/2, whoever you are
C_mar  <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1.5 * X))         # recorded less often when X is high (plogis is the logistic function)
C_mnar <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-1.5 * (Y - 1)))   # recorded less often when Y is high
c(mean(C_mcar), mean(C_mar), mean(C_mnar))

estimate <- function(C) {
  d <- data.frame(X = X, Y = ifelse(C, Y, NA))            # Y is NA where it is missing
  cc <- c(mean(d$Y, na.rm = TRUE), sd(d$Y, na.rm = TRUE) / sqrt(sum(C)))
  mean_imp <- ifelse(C, Y, mean(d$Y, na.rm = TRUE))        # fill the holes with the recorded mean
  mi <- c(mean(mean_imp), sd(mean_imp) / sqrt(n))
  reg_imp <- ifelse(C, Y, predict(lm(Y ~ X, data = d), newdata = d))   # fill the holes with the prediction from X
  ri <- c(mean(reg_imp), sd(reg_imp) / sqrt(n))
  imp <- mice(d, m = 20, method = "norm", printFlag = FALSE)   # 20 completions
  pooled <- summary(pool(with(imp, lm(Y ~ 1))))                # the mean of Y in each, pooled
  mult <- c(pooled$estimate, pooled$std.error)
  out <- rbind(`complete cases` = cc, `mean imputation` = mi,
               `regression imputation` = ri, `multiple imputation` = mult)
  colnames(out) <- c("estimate", "se")
  out
}
  1. Run the first block. Each indicator keeps about half the rows. Before you estimate anything, write down for each of the three indicators whether you expect the complete-case mean to be too high, too low, or right, and why.
  2. Run estimate() on each indicator and put the three tables side by side. The truth is \(1\); the standard error of the mean of all \(n\) values of \(Y\) would be about \(\sqrt{2/n} = 0.032\), and of half of them about \(0.045\).
  3. Read the tables by row. Which methods are biased under which mechanism? Which standard errors are too small, and what makes them too small? What does multiple imputation fix that regression imputation does not, and what does it not fix?
  4. Look inside mice. For the MAR data, imp <- mice(d, m = 20, method = "norm", printFlag = FALSE); then head(imp$imp$Y[, 1:4]) shows the first four completions’ values for the first few missing rows, and pool(with(imp, lm(Y ~ 1)))$pooled shows ubar (the average within-completion variance), b (the variance of the 20 estimates) and t (the total). Check that \(t = \bar u + (1 + 1/m)\,b\). Which of the three is what a single fill-in throws away?
  5. Every row of the MNAR column is wrong, multiple imputation included, because every method assumed that the missing \(Y\) are like the recorded \(Y\) with the same \(X\). An imputation model is an assumption about people who are not in the data. A statistics office imputes missing incomes in a survey that is used to set benefit levels. Who should choose the model, what should be published about it, and who would read it?
  6. At home: real data. R’s airquality (New York, May to September
    1. has 37 missing ozone readings out of 153. Tabulate the share missing by month. Estimate the mean ozone level by complete cases, mean imputation, and multiple imputation using Temp, Wind and Month. What does the multiple imputation assume about the days with missing ozone that no check on the data can confirm?

Problem 2. Selection on a collider, with numbers

A university admits by exam grade. Grades run from 1 to 10, each equally likely among applicants. Half the applicants come from a feeder school, independently of their grade. The pass mark is 7, and 5 for applicants from a feeder school. The university’s records cover admitted students only.

  1. Among admitted students, what is the mean grade of those from a feeder school, and of those not from one?

  2. Draw the causal graph with nodes grade, feeder school and admitted, and mark what the records condition on.

  3. An analyst concludes from the records that feeder schools produce weaker students, and the university considers withdrawing the lower pass mark. What is wrong, and who would pay for the mistake?

  4. Write down an admission rule under which, among the admitted, feeder-school students have the higher mean grade. What does this say about what the graph alone tells you, and about what the records are a record of?

TipHint 1

Among the admitted, which grades can a feeder-school student have, and which can a student from elsewhere have?

TipHint 2

For (d), make the feeder school’s bar the higher one.

Discussion 3: a label that exists only where a decision allowed it

A bank wants to know whether a new credit-scoring model would make better lending decisions than its current one. It evaluates the new model on its past applicants. It only knows whether an applicant repaid if it approved them.

  1. Who is missing from the evaluation, and by what mechanism? Which way are the new model’s measured error rates likely to be wrong?
  2. Who benefits from the problem going unnoticed?
  3. The honest fix is to approve some applicants the current model rejects, chosen at random, and record what happens. Who pays for that, and is it justified?

Bring one rule or score that sorts people into two groups: admit or reject, flag or clear, treat or not. Write down the two ways it can be wrong, and who pays for each. One sentence each.
