Scores and what they stand for

Class

Author

Joshua Loftus

Note

This is the student version of the class sheet. Parts marked (class) are for the class; the rest are practice. Answers are revealed in class.

In class

  1. A discussion of a score you know.
  2. Problems 1 to 3, by hand.
  3. A discussion of who should get the places.
  4. One simulation in R.

Discussions are in groups of three or four, then with the room. Each problem, and the simulation, is gone through as soon as the room has tried it. Everything under “Optional, can be done at home” is practice for afterward.

Discussion 1: a score you know

If you brought a score or algorithm used to make decisions about people, use it. If not, choose one: a credit score, a tool that screens CVs, a map that tells police where to patrol, the ranking of a social media feed, a university league table, the \(h\)-index.

  1. What does it measure, and what is it taken to stand for? Give one ethical consideration it raises.
  2. A common answer to “why do algorithms treat groups unequally?” is “the training data were biased.” Think of the hospital’s risk score from the lecture. Were its cost data wrong? If not, what was the problem?

Every group’s examples go on the screen.

Problems

Problems 1 to 3 can be done by hand.

1. An offset and who is selected

The lecture’s model: need \(Y^\star \sim N(0, 1)\) in both groups, \(A = 1\) for one group and \(A = 0\) for the other, the measure \(Y = Y^\star + bA\), and a person is selected if \(Y > t\). Now the program takes the top 10% of group 0, so \(t = 1.28\), and \(b = -0.5\). Use \(\Phi(1.28) = 0.900\), \(\Phi(1.78) = 0.9625\), \(\phi(1.28) = 0.1758\) and \(\phi(1.78) = 0.0818\), where \(\phi\) is the standard normal density.

  1. What share of each group is selected? What is the ratio of the two shares?

  2. What is the mean need of the selected in each group?

  3. Two people, one from each group, have the same score \(y\). How much more need does the person from group 1 have?

  4. Which group’s selected members are sicker? Say why in one sentence.

2. Noise and an omitted variable

From the lecture: health next year \(Y\), doctor visits \(X^\star\), reported as \(X = X^\star + \varepsilon\), and illness \(Z\), which is not in the data, with \[ Y = \beta X^\star + \gamma Z + U , \] where \(\varepsilon\) and \(U\) have mean zero and are independent of each other and of \(X^\star\) and \(Z\).

  1. Show that the slope of \(X^\star\) on \(X\) is \(\lambda = \operatorname{Var}(X^\star)/\operatorname{Var}(X)\). (The lecture’s best guess of need from cost is the same calculation.)

  2. Show that the slope of \(Y\) on \(X\) is \(\lambda(\beta + \gamma\delta)\), where \(\delta = \operatorname{Cov}(X^\star, Z)/\operatorname{Var}(X^\star)\).

  3. Take \(\beta = 1\) and \(\lambda = 0.9\). Compute the slope for \(\gamma\delta = 0.5\), \(-0.5\) and \(-1.5\). In each case, is it too large, too small or of the wrong sign?

  4. Can noise in the report change the sign of the slope? For which values of \(\gamma\delta\) is the slope in (c) larger than \(\beta\)?

  5. Which of \(\lambda\), \(\gamma\) and \(\delta\) can you learn from data on \(X\) and \(Y\) alone? What would you need to collect?

3. Who is selected

The hospital’s cost depends on more than need. Write a person’s cost as \[ Y = Y^\star + W + C + \varepsilon , \] with need \(Y^\star\); resources \(W\) (insurance that pays, income, transport, time off work, a doctor who records every diagnosis); care-seeking \(C\), how readily a person goes to a doctor at a given level of need; and noise \(\varepsilon\). The four are independent and normal with mean zero and variances \(1\), \(1/2\), \(1/4\) and \(1/4\). The program offers a place to everyone whose cost is in the top 3%: \(S = 1\) if \(Y > t\).

  1. Draw the graph with \(Y^\star\), \(W\), \(C\), \(Y\) and \(S\). Then sort \(Y^\star\), \(W\) and \(C\) by how far each is under a person’s own control.

  2. Show that \(E[Y^\star \mid Y = y] = \dfrac{\operatorname{Var}(Y^\star)}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}\, y\), and write the same for \(W\) and \(C\). What do the four fractions, with the one for \(\varepsilon\), add up to?

  3. The mean of a standard normal above its 97th percentile is 2.27. Compute the mean cost of the people offered a place, and the mean of each part among them. What share of their mean cost comes from need? What share from the part most under their control?

  4. Suppose poorer people are sicker: add an arrow from \(W\) to \(Y^\star\), with a negative effect. Without computing, does the mean need of the people offered a place go up or down? Why?

  5. In the original model, \(Y^\star\) and \(W\) are independent. Among the people offered a place, would you expect need and resources to be related? In which direction?

Discussion 2: who should get the places?

The people offered a place in problem 3 are a mixture: some are there mostly because they are ill, some because they could turn need into care, some because they seek care readily.

  1. Should it matter to the program why a person cleared the threshold? Would you treat a place that came from resources differently from one that came from seeking care readily?
  2. The lecture’s question was whether the program should go to the sickest or to those it would help most. Where do resources and care-seeking come in? Someone who seeks care readily may also be the most likely to use the program.
  3. Last week’s closing question, for everyone this time: does consequentialism become more or less defensible once you have to write the causal model down?

Simulation: who is selected

One laptop between two. The model of problem 3, for 100,000 people.

n <- 100000
need      <- rnorm(n)                     # Y*
resources <- rnorm(n, sd = sqrt(0.5))     # W
seeking   <- rnorm(n, sd = 0.5)           # C
noise     <- rnorm(n, sd = 0.5)
cost      <- need + resources + seeking + noise
selected  <- cost > quantile(cost, 0.97)  # the top 3% by cost are offered a place
parts <- data.frame(cost, need, resources, seeking, noise)
colMeans(parts[selected, ])
  1. Run it, and compare the means with your answers to problem 3(c).
  2. What share of the people offered a place are in the top 3% by need? mean(need[selected] > quantile(need, 0.97))
  3. Plot need against resources for the people offered a place. What do you see? Compare it with your answer to problem 3(e).
  4. Some people can raise their cost without being more ill: a doctor who records every diagnosis, knowing which tests to ask for. Say everyone with resources above the median adds 1 to their cost. Before you run anything, write down who you expect to gain places. Then change cost, run steps 1 and 2 again, and compare.
  5. At home: make poorer people sicker, need <- -0.5 * resources + rnorm(n, sd = sqrt(0.875)) (need still has variance 1), and run it again. Compare with problem 3(d).

4. A fixed number of places (harder)

In problem 1, suppose instead that the program has places for 10% of all patients, that the two groups are the same size, and that one threshold \(t\) applies to both.

  1. Write the equation that \(t\) solves. Is \(t\) above or below 1.28?

  2. Solve it in R with uniroot. What share of the places goes to group 1? With no offset it would be half.

  3. Compute the mean need of the selected in each group.

TipHint 1

The share of all patients selected is the average of the two groups’ shares.

5. Gaming or improvement

  1. From the lecture: learning the examiner’s favorite phrasing. Draw its graph. Is it gaming or improvement?

  2. A doctor who records every diagnosis. Draw the graph with need, the diagnoses recorded and cost. Is it gaming or improvement, and what does the answer depend on?

  3. Choose a score and one way people respond to it. Draw the graph and say which it is.

TipHint 1

A response is improvement if it raises what the score stands for, and the score with it; gaming if it raises the score by a route that does not pass through what the score stands for.

6. Prediction letters and reinforcement-learning letters

The decision whether to prescribe hormone therapy to prevent heart disease can be written with the letters used for prediction or with those of reinforcement learning.

  1. Write it with the letters used for prediction. Say what the treatment, the outcome and a confounder are, and write the quantity a doctor needs with \(do(\cdot)\).

  2. Write it with the letters of reinforcement learning, with the one-line declaration at the top. Say what the state, the action, the reward, the policy and the utility are.

  3. Which letters would mean different things if you used them in both answers?

TipHint 1

For (a), what does the doctor choose, and what could affect both that choice and heart disease? For (b), what does the doctor know when choosing?

Coding

B. Noise and an omitted variable

Problem 2’s model as a program.

n <- 100000
illness  <- rnorm(n)                                  # Z, not in the data
visits   <- illness + rnorm(n)                        # X*: the ill visit more
reported <- visits + rnorm(n, sd = 0.5)               # X, the number reported
health   <- 0.5 * visits - 1.5 * illness + rnorm(n)   # Y: beta = 0.5, gamma = -1.5
coef(lm(health ~ reported))
  1. Compute \(\lambda\), \(\delta\) and the slope you expect from problem 2. Then run it.
  2. Make the reports noisier, sd = 2. What do you expect? Run it.
  3. Fit lm(health ~ reported + illness). Is the coefficient on reported now \(\beta = 0.5\)? Why or why not?
TipHint 1

\(\operatorname{Var}(X^\star) = \operatorname{Var}(Z) + 1\) and \(\operatorname{Cov}(X^\star, Z) = \operatorname{Var}(Z)\).
